Every educational institution in the EU has a rather big choice to make in the next few months.  
  
It really clicked for me how big of a shit-storm is currently brewing after a conversation with a friend of mine in the final year of a humanities course. We were talking about the school year ahead, and he said something along the lines of *I can just Claude the whole year, bro*. I have unfortunately accepted the reality that most of my peers are outsourcing the education that they (or their family) pay for to a chatbot, but what concerns me especially is how many people like my friend are blissfully unaware of the switch Anthropic just flipped at their HQ.  
  
For those out of the loop, the [EU AI Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/)’s clause on text watermarking is set to go into effect in December:

> *Providers of AI systems, including general-purpose AI systems, generating synthetic audio, image, video or text content, shall ensure that the outputs of the AI system are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated. Providers shall ensure their technical solutions are effective, interoperable, robust and reliable as far as this is technically feasible, taking into account the specificities and limitations of various types of content, the costs of implementation and the generally acknowledged state of the art, as may be reflected in relevant technical standards. This obligation shall not apply to the extent the AI systems perform an assistive function for standard editing or do not substantially alter the input data provided by the deployer or the semantics thereof, or where authorised by law to detect, prevent, investigate or prosecute criminal offences.* 
>
>*Source: [artificialintelligenceact.eu](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/#p-article-50-2)*

In layman's terms, all major AI companies must have some form of state-of-the-art watermarking for generated text. The most popular of these implementations is [SynthID](https://deepmind.google/models/synthid/), a technology developed by Google's DeepMind lab. LLMs in general can be thought of as better versions of the Apple keyboard autocomplete; the model's task is to predict the next likely tokens (sets of characters) given the set of prior tokens. Watermarking systems like SynthID work by embedding a key into the choices that the LLM makes for each next token, which can then be recovered later as a means of detection. This process is probabilistic, meaning that what returns from these detectors is rather a *likelihood* than an exact gotcha. However, as the piece of generated text gets longer, the watermark token becomes more redundant across the text. At around 400 tokens (which roughly translates to about 500–600 English words depending on the model), SynthID showed an 85% true-positive-rate when the false-positive rate was 1%. This type of watermarking *can* be [obfuscated by enough manual editing](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.20228), but this proves more ineffective the longer the piece of text is. 

Anthropic, being the self-proclaimed safety champions of the bunch, went ahead and rolled their version of watermarking out at the end of September. At the time of writing, their companion detection tool is in private review, but crucially for this discussion, is open for use by educational organizations. This sets the stage for what I think anyone can see coming; in the next few months, there will be a large group of students in the EU submitting work with *I PLAGIARIZED THIS ASSIGNMENT* in invisible ink to professors with a UV light at the ready.  
  
A [large-scale study done in the US](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aec5115) this year used indirect questioning and maximum-likelihood to estimate that 9% of students were had used AI to cheat on an assignment. Specific majors such as economics and communications had an estimated cheating-rate as high as 17%. In my (unverified, un-researched) opinion, even these numbers feel quite conservative, but we’ll go with them for now. Assuming the roughly 1 in 10 students don’t reform their approach to education come the new year (and why would they if it's been working for them since 2022), universities in the EU will have to make the decision whether it’s worth putting up to a tenth of their student body on academic probation and/or suspension. Of course, there is an obvious moral choice here, but would you really trust every private institution in the EU to possibly risk throwing away up to 10% of tuition? Furthermore, how would any university's academic court handle that level of caseload, especially as there would probably be multiple cases per offending student? On the other end, if they decide not to investigate, or institute some type of grace period, what does that look like to the auditing bodies of accredited professional degrees? While industry tends to be more accepting and encouraging of "AI-enabled" work, even some CEOs are now speaking out about the [adversarial nature of careless LLM-use in the workplace](https://fortune.com/2026/09/17/shopify-tobias-lutke-ai-slop-grenades/). If this trend continues, I see many more professionals start to see the issue of a generation of grad workers engaging in a war of *slop-grenades* at the workplace.

This looks bad for undergraduate courses; I could see it being even worse for academic research. As [many](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01268) [studies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07016v5) have [shown](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.17036), LLM-styled writing became increasingly prevalent across multiple fields of research, and especially in computer science (who would have guessed?). The vast majority of leading academic journals [follow a disclosure-based policy](https://manusights.com/blog/journal-ai-policies-2026), where use of LLMs in research and writing must be declared, and while I have not done the research to back this up, I would be **very** surprised if every author flawlessly disclosed every time they got ChatGPT or Claude to write some of their papers' contents for them. In these cases, a university's reputation can now be impacted by the review of peers from *other* institutions by way of possible non-disclosure. However, compared to the student, I tend to have quite a lot more sympathy for the LLM-using researcher in this situation. Researchers, who have already been dealing with a culture of *publish or perish* within their institutions and fields for decades, are especially victim to being made what Cory Doctorow calls a [reverse-centaur](https://locusmag.com/feature/commentary-cory-doctorow-reverse-centaurs/) -- an LLM using a human as a means of operating in the flesh-world, and crucially, absorbing the accountability for its errors. As an IBM training manual in 1979 put it, [a computer can never be held accountable](https://feeds.simonwillison.net/2025/Feb/3/a-computer-can-never-be-held-accountable/).

Here's a depressing thought to end this post -- what if nobody cares? Whole industries (tech, consulting, tech-consulting, etc.) have already compiled to humans prompting LLMs at each other. This economy (and society in general) feels propped up by an attitude of *"it's not that deep, bro."* What if academia has already quietly accepted its submission into this automated loopery? If the research being generated by the LLMs is scientifically sound, does it matter? Does it also matter if the only ones who read them are LLMs prompted to summarize the text and its findings?

Big decisions.
