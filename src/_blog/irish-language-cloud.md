---
title: The Irish language is too important to keep in the cloud
subtitle: A less restrictive approach to software design and licensing could be a way forward for Irish online
date: 2026-09-23
tags: Irish Language, politics
lang: en
---

For the better part of two years, I’ve spent quite a large amount of time outside of college studying the Irish language. As any prospective Irish learner will tell you, a lot of studying time is spent usually hopping around a handful of reference websites. Some of these sites, such as [teanglann.ie](http://teanglann.ie), [corpas.ie](http://corpas.ie), and [abair.ie](http://abair.ie), have had availability issues over the past couple of months. This is not to pick on these sites, or those responsible for maintaining them. I would not be surprised if this drop in availability has in part to do with the Irish government time and time again choosing to [reduce funding for these programmes](https://www.forasnagaeilge.ie/budget-statement-2025-31-january-2025/). In addition, having done web administration work for websites, like the one you’re reading this on, I’m all too aware of how much harder the job has become in the advent of AI companies (often illegally) crawling every corner of the internet to train their models, with no regard for smaller sites’ ability to handle the traffic.

Unfortunately, situations like these are the best-case scenarios. As many prospective Irish learners will also tell you, the world of Irish online can feel like walking through a graveyard – a rather dramatic irony given the history of the language. Sites initially hosted through one-off public schemes often shut down due to a lack of funding or developer support. Many times this means the permanent loss of public access to the underlying data, a loss in both taxpayer funding and the hard work of those involved in the service’s creation. Given this government’s demonstrated inability to provide long-term, reliable support for online Irish-language services, we should put more effort into building resources that don’t rely on an internet connection in the first place.

Let me give an example of when local-first design works. A couple of months ago, I struggled immensely to determine the grammatical gender of a noun in Irish. Unfortunately, the signifier of a noun’s gender is often a combination of multiple loose rules, and each of these rules have quite a few exceptions to them. This made it an absolute mess to study on my own, and in my frustration, I dreamt up a game where I could be given a random noun, click the gender I think it is, and be scored on how well I guess it. Being the somewhat tech-savvy computer engineering student I am, I decided I’d try coding the game myself. After a bit of research, I found out that Foras na Gaeilge has a downloadable [National Morphology Database](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/gram/_download), which includes a list of every noun in the Irish language and its grammatical gender.

Given the database’s permissive [Open Database License](https://opendatacommons.org/licenses/odbl/1-0/), I was allowed to download this database, code up a program that randomly drew a noun from the list, and provide the user with buttons to guess the gender of the noun. This entire effort took less than two hours, and it has since been used by many other Irish learners online to help with their own language studies (you can find the game in [this repository](https://github.com/SeanMcGoff/cluiche-na-hinscne)). Even better, since the code for my game is under a very permissive license itself, someone else is able to download it and improve on it themselves. This is exactly what the developers of Irish-language social-media [Cogar](https://cogar.ie/) did when they added a version of my game to their app. This all would not have been legally possible if this data was behind a more restrictive licence or copyright protection.

That is where most of the issues of a local-first policy come down to – licensing. Many primary resources used by online services, such as dictionaries and thesauruses, are bound in copyright, meaning agencies wanting to digitise their contents have to enter exclusive licensing agreements with the copyright holders. For example, *Foclóir Gaeilge-Béarla*, an Irish-English dictionary edited by Niall Ó Dónaill, is one of the sources behind [teanglann.ie](http://teanglann.ie). Niall passed away in 1995, so the dictionary will only enter public domain [70 years after his death in 2065](https://www.ipoi.gov.ie/en/types-of-ip/copyright1/understanding-copyright/duration-of-copyright-protection/). Until then, anyone who wants to legally build something for the Irish language on top of that dictionary will have to either contact the estate of the author, or wait 40 years. I don’t want this to seem as if I don’t want the authors or their families to get properly compensated for their work in the Irish language; I do! However, it would be in the public interest for public bodies to work with the authors and academics behind these resources to free up licensing. This will ensure the entire Irish-language community can benefit from new tools and resources that don’t necessarily require an internet connection.

Furthermore, I’d argue that the current licensing situation is doing a disservice to the creators of the resources as well. In June of this year, Pól Deeds, the Irish language commissioner, went on RTÉ *Raidió na Gaeltachta* (Radio of the Gaeltacht, RnaG) show *Barrscéalta* and [raved about the use of AI](https://tuairisc.ie/an-intleacht-shaorga-molta-ag-an-gcoimisineir-gaeilge-dudarais-phoibli/) as a means of providing Irish-language services. As mentioned earlier, AI companies have been using rather invasive web-crawlers to compile corpora to train their models, which in no doubt include both the copyrighted primary resources, and websites derived from them. Not only does government funding seem to dry up for cloud services built on these primary resources, but government members are now actively pushing technology that cuts out an agreement with the resource licensors entirely.

The history of the Irish language is one largely defined by defiance. Historically, that defiance was based in grassroots efforts to pass the language on despite colonial rule. I would argue that that spirit still lives on in the digital realm. I would not be at the level of Irish I’m at now without grassroots networks delivering sketchy PDF scans of hard-to-find books, or people making guerrilla translations of pronunciation guides locked behind copyright. If government, academia, and the learning community worked together to design a more equitable way to license Irish-language resources, the entire community could benefit from what could be created. Those before us refused to accept a reality without Irish in this country, we shouldn’t have to accept the present reality of Irish online either.
